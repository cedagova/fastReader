A Word at a Time

The lighthouse keeper's daughter learned to read in flashes.

Her father's tower stood on a rock that the mainland could only see on clear days, and the light above her head turned all night long, sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs. Books came out to the rock four times a year, in a crate lined with oilcloth, and between one crate and the next she read the same forty volumes until she knew their weather by heart.

She read them at the top of the tower, in the machine room, where the only light was the light itself. Every eleven seconds the beam swung past the window and the page went white. In between there was nothing to see at all.

So she learned to take a page in pieces. One flash, one word. Then darkness, and the word turned itself over in her head while she waited for the next one. Eleven seconds is a long time to hold a single word. She found that some of them could not survive it — the, of and and fell apart almost at once — and that others grew heavier the longer she held them. Salt. Rope. Forgive.

Her father thought it was a slow way to read. He was right, and it did not matter. By the time she was fifteen she could carry a sentence across four flashes and set it down whole on the other side.

When the mainland finally sent a boat for her, she found that ordinary reading felt like being handed the whole ocean at once. Pages arrived complete, every word shouting together, and none of them had time to become anything. She would put the book down, look at the wall, and wait, out of habit, for the dark.

She never did get that habit back out of herself. She read slowly all her life, one word at a time, and she was the only person any of her friends knew who could still quote, forty years later, every line of the forty books that came out to the rock.
